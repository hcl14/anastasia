Doctor#08

The question is technical: electrodes or applicators for radiofrequency thermal ablation (RFA) need to be placed there. For electrochemotherapy, it is technologically difficult to treat a volumetric process, because the depth of loading of the electrodes into the tumor is only 4-5 mm, 6 mm maximum. Their zone of action will not cover the entire tumor volume. Therefore, purely from the technical point of view, radiofrequency thermal ablation would be the best fit here.

But to assess feasibility, the person who guides them for placement of the applicators (in this case, for us this is an ultrasound diagnostic doctor, she works as an ultrasound navigator) needs to see online where we are passing the applicator. In essence, it is a needle-like bipolar electrode. We need to find a safe trajectory for passing this trocar and understand in what stereotactic position we will place them, whether we can place the applicators into the target zone or cannot. For this, the ultrasound doctor needs to examine the patient.

Patient representative#09

Doctor#10

We need CT in order to orient ourselves and correlate the data. We assess that there is a certain number of foci in the liver, and among these foci we need to choose the one that is more active at the present time, and specifically place this focus. Therefore we will need to correlate the data of computed tomography or PET-CT. Since in this conversation we are orienting ourselves by PET-CT, that there is this active focus of 3-4 cm there, we need to assess how safe it is to work with this focus near a large vessel. For the most part, large vessels are not injured by RFA, because blood circulation in them works as heat removal in a constant mode. Accordingly, RFA is not so dangerous for a large vessel, and there one can vary the distance of the electrode's active zone of action relative to this large vessel. But we need to understand whether, at such an angle, we can pass through tissues through the skin or liver and safely place this applicator or applicators into the focus. And for this, the patient herself needs to be assessed.

Doctor#18

In tumors it usually does not happen that way. A mutation occurs not in one cell, but in thousands or millions of cells of a specific tumor. It is unlikely that one tumor has only one mutation and another has another. Cells with a certain mutation or with another mutation may predominate in this specific tumor. If against the background of the appearance of these mutations you see that two foci are progressing, then they are probably connected precisely with this mutation that is now escaping from the effect of the existing treatment. Patient representative#27

Doctor#28

RFA causes the temperature in tissues to rise to 60-90 degrees. The tissue in the applicator's zone of action is essentially cooked. This is a zone of injury, which through biological mechanisms is realized as necrosis. Usually a kind of volumetric scar forms (a zone of fibrosis, replaced by connective tissue). Cyst formation is rare.

Patient representative#29

Doctor#30

Intoxication exists. Therefore separate foci need to be selected: if we ablate a large amount of tissue, this will lead to resorption syndrome (substances from necrosis will enter the blood). But for foci of 3-8 cm, patients tolerate this quite well. Body temperature may rise (37.2 - 38.8°C), liver parameters may react (transaminases, ALT, AST, gamma-glutamyltransferase).
