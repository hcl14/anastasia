Risks of the two-stage approach: progression between stages

Dr Fedorov — opinion

Risks named

  • Drop-out before stage two because of progression: a risk that, unfortunately, cannot be excluded.
  • Operative risk assessed as moderate but real: complications and post-operative liver failure may occur.
  • Gastric resection adds its own risks (see above).
  • Complications, even when successfully managed, lengthen the interval between the operations and so create a risk of progression.
  • Progression to unresectability between the stages cannot be ruled out.
  • There are risks of departing from the two-stage plan.
  • As far as he understands the oncologists, they see no alternative other than this operation with these risks.

Why removing even one stage is worthwhile

  • To the Polish clinic's view that treating as much as possible "prolongs", he said "partly so", because this tumour grows slowly; it grows exponentially — slowly at first, then faster.
  • For this tumour the total tumour burden in the body really matters.
  • Even if only the right lobe is removed and she then drops out of the second stage, that is still a survival benefit from the reduced tumour mass.
  • Of course, it is better to remove everything.

Progression versus new metastases

  • He distinguished two terms: progression is enlargement of existing metastases; metastasising is the formation of new distant foci.
  • She already has metastatic disease: the liver tumour is not primary but a metastasis.
  • New metastases are potentially possible, and local treatment of existing ones cannot prevent them.
  • Metastasising is a systemic process driven by immune deficit and the activity of the primary tumour; it can be influenced only by systemic therapy.
  • What the family probably means is progression (growth of what exists); it is indeed possible and cannot be safely prevented.

Left-lobe lesion: no embolisation or ablation now

Dr Fedorov — opinion

Embolisation

  • It is unclear what to embolise. Hepatocellular carcinoma is fed mainly by the artery, so embolising the artery works there; this is neither that nor a colorectal metastasis, and this tumour tends, on the contrary, towards necrosis.
  • Embolisation during the operation is possible at the Centre, but he does not think it should be done: there is no advantage to doing it intra-operatively.

Ablation

  • Local-control methods are either resection (removing everything) or ablation (overheating the tumour).
  • There are ablation procedures: in essence a local microwave oven — a radiofrequency antenna overheats the tissue, the burn persists after the current stops, and the result is local aseptic necrosis that usually causes no major intoxication.
  • It works well, and is usually effective, for small lesions that one or several electrodes can cover; the field of each electrode is roughly spherical and burns out everything within it.
  • Larger lesions need several electrodes placed to cover the whole tumour geometrically, which in her case would be very hard — only a partial, not a complete, ablation.
  • Ablation would work only if everything could be burned out at once, which is not possible here; attempting it would most likely be equivalent to the same resection.
  • That is exactly where the danger lies.
  • Ideally her lesions should be removed surgically, if possible.
  • He would regard ablation as a palliative procedure when other options are impossible.

Treating the left lesion during the first operation

  • Treating it during stage one usually adds toxic or inflammatory load that could decompensate the liver.
  • Not impossible, but risky: an additional risk of immediate post-operative complications.
  • He does not think local treatment of the left-lobe metastasis is advisable now, because whatever is done it cannot be treated radically by local means; at most part of it would be ablated.
  • Partial necrosis releases pro-inflammatory and aggressive growth factors on a massive scale; the remaining tumour's reaction is unknown.

If the left lesion grows before stage two

  • The plan would be palliative ablations or chemoembolisations, or whatever other alternative methods can offer.
  • Most likely this would not be radical.
  • Asked whether this could buy time until the next operation, he said it could.

Immune stimulation: not indicated

Dr Fedorov — opinion
  • Usually the only way to stimulate immunity is immunotherapy (making the tumour visible to the immune system), and it is not indicated in her case.
  • General immune activation does not work: immunity cannot be switched on and off or turned up like a dial.
  • Immunomodulators: usually unproven drugs causing no change or, worse, unstudied changes.
  • Any cancer reflects an imbalance between the immune system's normal clearing of cancer cells (which arise even in healthy people) and the number of such cells and the quality of immune surveillance; surveillance and immunodeficiency have very many subtypes.
  • Studying and influencing immunity early would probably solve the problem; no such possibility is known to him today.
  • On in-vivo CAR-T: it would be great if something like that appeared.

Tumour tissue: specimen goes to pathology; storage to be arranged with the pathologist

Dr Fedorov — opinion
  • Biobank questions should go to Vladyslav Kropelnytskyi.
  • The surgeons' responsibility ends with handing the specimen, unaltered, to pathology; pathology decides whether part of it goes elsewhere.
  • Biobanking is something different: a company collects biological samples, de-identifies them and uses them for its own research; it is not part of the patient's treatment, and probably not what the family means.
  • Freezing in liquid nitrogen is just a fixation method; the question is what happens to the specimen afterwards.
  • Biobanking is a clinical study in which a patient may take part voluntarily, usually for benefits from the company, which might for example cover part of the patient's medical treatment or provide something else.
  • Such systems existed "with us" before; he does not know whether they exist now ("with us" = at the Centre or in Ukraine; see "Wording unclear").
  • It does not affect treatment; research findings do not come back to the patient.
  • Talk to the pathologist; if there are specific requirements for the specimen, the surgical team can meet them.
  • On the family's idea of CSD or another private laboratory, he replied only "talk" (to whom not stated).
